there's a line from nirmal purja, nimsdai, that i keep coming back to: "giving up is not in the blood, sir."

some background, because it earns him the sentence. gurkha, then uk special forces, then high-altitude mountaineer. in 2019 he climbed all fourteen of the world's 8,000-meter peaks in six months and six days. the previous record was almost eight years.

it's easy to file that under motivation-reel material. i think it's more useful than that, but only if you take it apart properly.

## where the mantra is literally true

above 8,000 meters, climbers call it the death zone. there isn't enough oxygen to keep a human alive for long. your body is on a timer the moment you cross the line.

which changes what "giving up" means. down here, quitting is a decision. up there, it's a physical event. the moment you surrender to the cold and sit down, you stop generating heat, and the mountain starts closing the account.

so when nimsdai says you give up, you die, he isn't performing intensity. he's describing the physics of his workplace. up there, the mind has to outrank the body, because the body's advice, sit down, rest, stop, is fatal.

that's also what his rescues were about. mid-record, he repeatedly diverted to carry down climbers who had stopped moving. on the mountain, one person's surrender becomes everyone's emergency.

## what travels down the mountain

a few things from that world translate cleanly to ours.

**there is always a way.** his military habit: refuse panic first, then look for the route. most stuck situations have more exits than the panicking version of you can see.

**the body lies under load.** whatever you're carrying, the first scream of "i can't" is rarely the truth. it's a negotiation opening. people who know this get a second tank most people never discover.

**your extremes, someone's normality.** the ceiling you treat as fixed is somebody's tuesday. that alone is worth sitting with. limits are more adjustable than they feel from inside.

## the line the posters skip

and now the part that the motivational edit leaves out.

you are, most days, not in a death zone. you're in a job, a startup, a relationship, a degree. and down here, "never give up" gets sold as if every mountain deserves your fingers and toes.

it doesn't. sunk cost is real. some summits are just the wrong summit, and the years you'd spend proving you're not a quitter are years billed against the life you actually want.

mountaineers themselves have a rule the reels never print: the summit is optional. coming home is not. the best climbers in the world turn around meters from the top when the math goes bad. that's not weakness. that's the judgment that lets them climb again.

## grit for moments, judgment for directions

so here's how i've reconciled the two, for myself.

when the pain is a moment, an exam week, a hard launch, the last stretch of something you chose for good reasons, run nims mode. don't negotiate with the fatigue. keep moving. that voice isn't information, it's weather.

when the doubt is about the direction, the career that stopped making sense, the relationship running on history, the goal you inherited instead of chose, don't run nims mode. sit down, breathe, and do the math like a mountaineer at the turnaround point. is this summit worth what it's charging?

don't quit because it's hard. quit if it isn't worth it. those are different sentences, and most bad decisions come from confusing them.

so next time you want to stop, ask which voice is talking. if it's fatigue, keep walking. if it's the math, be reasonable. both answers, on the right day, are the strong one.
