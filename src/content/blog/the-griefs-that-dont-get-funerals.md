grief has a dress code in most people's heads. a funeral. a condolence message. a reason everyone can see.

but most of the grief i've watched people carry, and carried myself, never got any of that. nothing died, exactly. so nobody brought flowers.

a psychologist named kenneth doka gave this a name back in 1989. disenfranchised grief. the grief you're not officially allowed to have, because the loss doesn't count as a loss on paper.

no ritual. no leave from work. no one showing up with food. just you, quietly mourning something other people can't see.

i started collecting these. not to wallow in them. because naming a thing is half of carrying it well. here are the ones i keep meeting.

## the almosts

**the thing you almost had.** you were close enough to start imagining the life that came with it. the job, the person, the deal. then it didn't happen.

everyone around you moved on in a week, because to them it was just a thing that didn't work out. to you it was everything you had quietly been building towards. and months later, sometimes years later, it still visits. in the background. unannounced.

**not being chosen.** for the job. for the relationship. for the friendship. by the parent who had a favourite, and it wasn't you.

people minimize this one constantly. something better is coming, it wasn't meant for you. maybe they're right. but before the better thing, before the perspective, there is just the plain ache of not being the one they picked. that ache deserves a minute before the silver linings arrive.

**the dream you let go of quietly.** at some point, without announcing it, you stopped talking about a certain thing you wanted. stopped including it in your plans. made peace with it not happening. or told yourself you did.

then you see someone else living the version of the life you used to imagine, and something moves in you that you've learned to push down fast. that thing you push down is grief. it deserves a nod, even if you've genuinely chosen a different road.

## the time

**knowing you're capable of more.** you can see the gap between where you are and where you know you could be. that awareness is its own quiet torture, because you can't unknow it.

but the starting point is blurry and the path is unclear, and you're stuck between the knowing and the doing in a way that exhausts you. it's grief for a version of you that exists, so far, only in your own head.

**the years that don't add up.** time given to the wrong relationship. to people who didn't value it. seasons where you weren't moving, weren't growing, weren't building anything you're proud of.

you look back and the math doesn't feel good. and even though every experience taught you something, there are days when the lesson doesn't feel worth the fee. this one stings because time is the one thing that can't be returned.

**the person you thought you'd be by now.** the timeline you set. the standards you had for yourself, good ones, reasonable ones. and then life didn't cooperate, and now you're here. which is fine. which is okay. which is not what you pictured.

grieving the gap between who you planned to be and who you actually are might be the most private grief a person can carry. almost nobody says it out loud.

## the people

**the relationship that ended before you were ready.** even if it was the right call. even if you can list every reason it couldn't work. even if you were the one who ended it.

people expect you to be fine, because the decision was logical, because time has passed, because you seem okay. but grief doesn't follow logic. you can know something wasn't right for you and still mourn the version of your life where it was.

**outgrowing people you love.** you didn't stop loving them. you grew, and the space between where you are and where they are got too wide to pretend across. conversations that used to flow now feel thin. connection that used to be easy now takes effort that feels a little dishonest.

what you're grieving isn't the person. it's the version of the relationship that used to fit, before you needed different things than you needed before. growing up sometimes means growing apart, and that loss is real even when nobody did anything wrong.

**the parent you have but can't reach.** they're alive. they're present. and there's still a gap that never fully closed. a conversation that was never had. a version of being known by them that you always wanted and never quite got.

this one is complicated because people don't understand it. they say at least you still have them. but you're grieving something inside the relationship itself. the closeness that was never quite there, and that you kept hoping would come.

**grieving people who are still here.** there's a name for this too. anticipatory grief. it starts the day you notice your parents visibly aging, and some part of you begins mourning while they're still on the other end of the phone.

it's the 7am call from home that has you panicking before you even pick up. and when it turns out to be your dad just saying hello, you find yourself yelling at him out of pure leftover fear, telling him never to call that early again.

it carries guilt too. the worry that if something happens, you won't have done enough for them. that they won't have had enough time enjoying life with you. you're grieving a loss that hasn't happened, and it shapes your days anyway.

## the self

**the childhood that was fine and still wasn't enough.** not necessarily a traumatic one. sometimes just the slow realization that certain things you needed weren't available. that the child you were deserved more softness, more stability, more being seen.

you can love your parents and still grieve parts of your upbringing. you can have had a fine childhood by most standards and still mourn something that was missing. both things are allowed to be true at the same time.

**who you were before things got hard.** you remember a lighter version of yourself. more spontaneous. less guarded. someone who laughed without calculating, trusted without evidence, dreamed without immediately listing the reasons it wouldn't work.

life happened, and that version of you had to adapt. you're stronger now, sure. but sometimes you grieve the person before the lessons. before the walls. before the world taught them to be careful.

**what surviving cost you.** you're out of it now. the toxic situation, the hard season, the stretch of life that was just about making it through the day. you should feel relief, and sometimes you do.

but there's also this strange, quiet grief for what that period took. the years. the energy. the version of you that got used up just staying afloat. you're not there anymore, but you carry the weight of having been there.

**the body you used to live in.** illness. weight changes. aging. hormonal shifts. a body that used to do something it no longer does.

there's real grief in watching your physical self change, especially when the change wasn't your choice. people rarely name it as grief, but it is. the mourning of a body you used to move through more freely, more without thinking. you can practice acceptance and still grieve what changed. those aren't mutually exclusive either.

## what to do with all this

none of these come with a ritual. that's the whole problem. the world files them under "things that happen" and expects you to keep walking.

so make a small ritual yourself. say it out loud, to a friend or just to your own room. this was real. i lost something. it counts.

not to stay in it. just to stop pretending it isn't there. named grief moves through you. unnamed grief just moves in.

you don't need permission to mourn something that mattered to you. but if it helps, consider this the permission.
