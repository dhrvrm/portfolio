at six in the morning on 13 november, koramangala was still dark. i rolled my first bike, mayeri, out onto the road and pointed her at mangaluru.

five days later i was home. the odometer said 1,156 km.

here's the part i didn't say out loud at the time. i was still learning to ride.

## most people learn in traffic

the usual way to learn a bike in india is the city. you wobble through intersections, get honked at, get cut off, and slowly your hands figure it out somewhere between a bus and an auto.

i did it backwards. my hands did their learning on the ghats.

the climbs and descents of shiradi, the coastal road out of udupi with the sea on my left, the sharp drops into gokarna's beaches, the fog outside shivamogga at six in the morning. that was the classroom.

it sounds reckless when i write it like that. it wasn't, and the reason it wasn't is the part i keep thinking about.

## the hands were new. the eyes weren't.

i came to this trip with new hands and old eyes.

i'd been on indian roads my whole life. pillion on a splendor to kedarnath. years of bengaluru traffic. enough time watching how an intersection actually behaves, versus how the signage says it should, that i'd even written about it.

so the intuitions were already there. which gap a car will take before the driver knows it. how sand collects on the inside of a curve. when a truck's wheels say it's about to drift. what the road surface is about to do, read from its colour.

what i didn't have was the muscle. the clutch finesse on a steep climb. how much front brake is too much mid-corner. where to put my eyes through a descending hairpin.

those are two different kinds of knowing, and the trip made the difference very plain. one of them transfers from years of watching. the other one only comes through your hands, kilometre by kilometre.

## a beautiful road is a generous teacher

shiradi ghat had butterflies everywhere. so many that i felt like i'd ridden into a disney film. greenery, the sound of water, and these moments of, oh man, i am actually here, doing this.

you'd think that's a distraction. you're learning to ride, keep your eyes on the tarmac, not the butterflies.

i found the opposite.

the beauty kept me awake in a way traffic never has. in the city, attention is defensive. you're bracing, filtering noise, waiting for the next thing to lunge at you. it wears you down, and worn-down attention gets sloppy.

on the ghat, attention felt like the whole point. the same alertness that caught the butterflies caught the gravel in the curve. i wasn't bracing. i was looking, at everything, and the road was part of everything.

i had joshi bhaiya's voice notes in my head, explaining how to take exactly these climbs and descents. when a section went smoothly i'd be quietly impressed with myself, then straight back to the next one.

that loop, try, notice, adjust, is just learning. the ghat ran it for me every ninety seconds.

## the road kept checking my homework

it wasn't all butterflies.

on the way to mangaluru, an innova slipped on a patch of road ahead of me, wheels locked, and spun about half a circle. i stopped with other people to help. everyone was okay.

standing there, i understood something at body level that i'd only known at trivia level before. that patch of road was readable. the information was there before the slip.

after that i rode differently. not slower, exactly. more deliberately. i started treating the surface as something that was talking to me the whole time, and my job was to not stop listening.

each day set a different exam. friday was the mountains. sunday was the coast, long and smooth and empty, where the danger is that nothing demands anything of you. the approach to gokarna got sharp and steep just when i'd relaxed. tuesday was fog so thick and cold that my visor's anti-fog insert earned its whole price in one morning.

by the time i rolled back into koramangala, my hands had caught up with my eyes.

## what i'd tell someone starting

i'm not going to tell you to skip the city and go learn in the mountains. that innova could have been me, and the margin on a ghat is thinner than the margin outside your house.

but i'll say this. where you learn something shapes how you learn it.

if the only place you practice is a place that punishes you, you learn to brace. if you can find a place that rewards attention, pays it back in butterflies and sea and light, you learn to look. and looking, it turns out, is most of the skill.

that's true of more than bikes. i learn better in codebases i find beautiful too. same loop. attention first, hands catch up.

mayeri and i did 1,156 km, and somewhere in the middle of them i stopped being someone who was learning to ride and started being someone who rides.

i couldn't point to the kilometre where it happened. i just know the butterflies were there for the before, and the fog was there for the after.

where did your hands learn the thing your eyes already knew? i'm curious whether it was a kind place or a harsh one.
